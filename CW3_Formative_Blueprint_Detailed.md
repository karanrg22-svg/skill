# 3. The harsh marker's mind

## 3.1 What a harsh marker is silently asking on every sentence

1. **So what?** Does this sentence make a claim, or just state a fact?
2. **Says who?** Is the claim supported by a source or by specific evidence?
3. **Compared with what?** Does the student weigh this idea against an alternative?
4. **Whose view?** Is the source independent, or is it the leader describing themselves?
5. **Where does it break?** Does the student see the limits of their own theory and choice?
6. **Is it yours?** Does it sound like a specific person with a specific experience, or a template?

## 3.2 What earns trust (include)

| Signal | How it shows on the page |
|---|---|
| Position-taking | Paragraphs open with a claim, not a topic label. |
| Theory as tool | A named concept is linked to one concrete behaviour or case. |
| Critique with a citation | Each major theory has a named critic or limitation, cited. |
| Specific evidence | An incident, a feedback comment, a score, a time. |
| Honest weakness | A real gap, stated precisely, with its effect on other people. |
| Evolution | "Before → trigger → after" is visible in thinking. |
| Alignment | Section order mirrors the brief (AC1, then AC3; strengths, then development areas). |
| Precision | Correct names, years and terms; no inflated claims. |
| Reference list quality | Mixed, credible, correctly formatted, and every entry used. |
| Self-aware limits | The plan names its own risks (for example, biased sources). |

## 3.3 What irritates (remove)

| Irritant | Why it costs you | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Opening with a dictionary definition | Description, no argument | Open with the contested point instead |
| Theory lists | Shows reading, not thinking | Justify *why these* in a clause |
| Vague self-praise ("good communicator", "passionate") | Unevidenced, generic | Replace with an incident and its effect |
| "Studies show…" with no citation | Plagiarism risk, weak credibility | Name the author and year |
| Unverified numbers | Can be wrong, and may be checked in a viva | Do not use a figure you haven't read |
| Repeating the brief | Wastes words | Go straight to content |
| Celebrity leader choice with no rationale | Looks like popularity, not analysis | Criterion-based selection (section 10) |
| Using only the leader's autobiography | Source bias | Triangulate with independent sources |
| Reference list padding | Marker will spot unused entries | Cite everything, list only what you cited |
| Over-polished reflection with no discomfort | Reads as untrue | Include one genuinely uncomfortable insight |
| Jargon without definition | Signals surface knowledge | Define in one clause or drop it |
| Passive, hedged prose ("it could be argued that perhaps") | Weak voice | Take a position and qualify it precisely |

# 6. TASK 1 blueprint: Reflective Learning (AC4), 300 words

## 6.0 Strategy in one paragraph

Your reflection should read as **one argument about yourself**, not a diary. The argument has a shape: *here is a specific thing I did in Week 4; here is what theory says it reveals (and where theory is inadequate); here is my strongest asset and my most costly gap; here is how my understanding changed; here is my verdict and what I will do*. It should sound like a person thinking, with the discomfort left in.

**Choose one reflective frame and stay inside it.** Options (⚠ read before citing):

| Frame | Structure | Best if |
|---|---|---|
| Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001) | What? So what? Now what? | You want brevity. It maps neatly onto 300 words. **Recommended.** |
| Gibbs (1988) | Description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan | You want a more formal cycle but have less room |
| Schön (1983) | Reflection-in-action versus on-action | Your example is a real-time decision |

If you use a frame, mention it in a clause, not a paragraph. The marker cares about the thinking, not the label.

## 6.1 Move 1: Situation and thesis (30 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Ground the reflection in one real Week 4 moment and state your overall judgement upfront. |
| Must contain | (a) a specific moment, result or feedback from the Week 4 activity; (b) a one-sentence thesis about your leadership. |
| Must avoid | Describing the activity itself ("In Week 4 we were asked to…"). The marker already knows it. |
| Evidence needed | YOU SUPPLY: the actual moment, score or comment. |
| Quality test | Could another student have written this sentence? If yes, make it more specific. |
| Thesis shapes | "My strength is X, but it conceals Y." / "I believed X; the activity showed Y." The second links to your evolution move. |

## 6.2 Move 2: Theoretical analysis of behaviour (95 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Explain *why you behaved as you did* using theory, then test the theory. |
| Must contain | One named framework mapped to one behaviour; a statement of the component (for example, which Goleman competency); a limit or critique, cited. |
| Recommended lens A | Goleman (1998) five EI components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skill. Pick the **one or two** that your evidence supports. |
| Recommended lens B | Goleman (2000) leadership styles (for example affiliative, democratic, coaching, pacesetting, coercive, authoritative). Name the style your behaviour resembled, and say whether it *fit the situation*. |
| Required critique | Antonakis, Ashkanasy and Dasborough (2009): EI as a construct is questioned (measurement, overlap with personality and intelligence, evidence of incremental value). ⚠ Read this paper and cite the specific point you use. |
| Optional contrast | Mayer and Salovey (1997) ability model or Petrides and Furnham (2001) trait EI, to show that "EI" is not one thing. |
| Must avoid | Listing all five competencies. Defining EI. Saying "I demonstrated empathy" without showing how. |
| Sentence functions (in order) | 1. Behaviour (one clause). 2. Theory it maps to (one clause + citation). 3. Why it maps (the reasoning). 4. What the theory fails to capture (critique + citation). |
| Quality test | Remove the theory name. Does the sentence still make sense? If so, the theory is decoration. Re-write so the theory carries the explanation. |

## 6.3 Move 3: Evaluation: strength and development area (70 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Show balanced self-evaluation with evidence and consequences. |
| Must contain | One strength and one development area; for each: evidence, effect on others, and a link to theory. |
| Structure | Strength (about 30 words): *what, evidence, effect*. Gap (about 40 words): *what, evidence, effect, why it matters for leadership*. The gap deserves more space. |
| The "uncomfortable insight" | Include one genuinely uncomfortable observation. Examples of the *type* (not for copying): a strength that overreaches into a weakness; a trait that builds rapport but avoids hard conversations; high empathy that delays decisions. Choose only what is true for you. |
| Link ideas | Trust (Mayer, Davis and Schoorman, 1995: ability, benevolence, integrity) and psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) are useful for explaining *consequences for others*. ⚠ read before use. |
| Must avoid | Humble-bragging (a "weakness" that is really a strength, such as "I care too much"). A harsh marker spots this instantly. |
| Quality test | Does the gap cost somebody something specific? If it costs nobody anything, it isn't a real gap. |

## 6.4 Move 4: How your understanding evolved (50 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Show *progression over time*, which the rubric explicitly names. |
| Must contain | A **before** belief, a **trigger** (an activity insight, feedback, or reading), and an **after** position that changes behaviour or thinking. |
| Depth marker | Show a change in *understanding* (what leadership is), not only a change in skill. For example, moving from "empathy equals being nice" to "empathy is information used to make better decisions". Use only what is true for you. |
| Must avoid | "I have learnt a lot." A before/after with no trigger. A change that is trivially obvious. |
| Quality test | Could the marker identify the exact thing that changed your mind? |

## 6.5 Move 5: Judgement and forward link (50 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Give an evidence-based verdict on your leadership potential and preview the AC4 development plan. |
| Must contain | A balanced verdict (with the reasoning visible); one or two specific actions. |
| Action standard | Specific, measurable, time-bound, and tied to the gap in Move 3. For example: *what behaviour, in what setting, by when, and how I will know it worked*. Use SMART thinking without writing the acronym. |
| Verdict wording | Conditional and honest: "potential in X, provided I Y". Not "I will become a great leader". |
| Realism | Actions must be feasible for a student (team projects, placement, society role). Avoid grand promises. |
| Link to AC4 | One clause that shows this will be developed into a justified development plan in the summative. |

## 6.6 Task 1 cutting order (if over 300)

1. CUT FIRST: any definition of EI.
2. CUT FIRST: scene-setting in Move 1 beyond the specific moment.
3. Reduce the strength to one clause; keep the gap.
4. Reduce to one action instead of two.
5. Never cut: the critique, the evidence for the gap, or the before/after.

# 8. TASK 2 blueprint: Summative coursework plan, 500 words

## 8.0 Strategy in one paragraph

The marker is judging whether you can already think like an AC1/AC3 writer. A top-quality plan is not a list of intentions. It is a **compressed argument**: it states a position, justifies the choices behind it, and shows you already know where the difficulties lie. Every choice (theory, example, leader, lens, source) must come with a *because*.

**Suggested headings (short, to save words):** *Overview*, *AC1: Understanding Leadership*, *AC3: Diverse Leader*, *Integration and risks*. Do not add further headings.

## 8.1 Orientation (25 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | State the single argument that the summative will make, so everything below has a purpose. |
| Must contain | A thesis-style statement connecting leadership theory to the diverse leader analysis. |
| Shape | "I will argue that [claim about leadership/management] and test it through [leader], using [lens]." |
| Must avoid | "This plan will outline…" |

## 8.2 AC1 Move 1: Theories and models (120 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Show which theories you will use to define leadership **and** management, why these, and where they are contested. |
| Must contain | (a) A clear leadership/management distinction source; (b) at least one approach model for leadership; (c) a critique; (d) a clause on *why this combination*. |
| Recommended spine | **Distinction:** Zaleznik (1977), Kotter (1990). **Reality check:** Mintzberg (1973). **Approach:** Burns (1978) and Bass (1985) on transformational versus transactional leadership. **Critique:** Alvesson and Spicer (2012) or another critical leadership source you have read. |
| Argument to make | The leadership/management split is useful analytically but blurred in practice. Mintzberg shows managers perform interpersonal and informational roles, so a good working definition acknowledges overlap. |
| Selection reasoning | Say why: for example, the classic distinction frames the debate, Mintzberg tests it, and the transformational model supplies a lens that connects to AC3. |
| Must avoid | Defining both terms in dictionary style. Listing five theories you won't use. Treating Kotter or Zaleznik as settled truth. |
| Sentence functions (in order) | 1. Your working position on leadership versus management. 2. The sources that support it (with a clause on each). 3. The challenge to it. 4. How you will resolve or use the tension. |
| Quality test | Could a reader tell what you *disagree with or qualify*? If not, it is a summary. |

## 8.3 AC1 Move 2: Real-world example (60 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Name at least one real example and say what it will let you test. |
| Recommended | Satya Nadella at Microsoft (⚠ verify facts and timeframe): the cultural shift under his leadership offers a test case for leadership versus management and for the role of empathy and growth mindset (Dweck, 2006). |
| Why a different person from AC3 | Avoids overlap and shows range. It also saves you re-explaining a context. |
| Sources to pair | Nadella (2017) *Hit Refresh* (a leader-authored account) **plus** at least one independent academic or business-press source. State this pairing in a clause to show source awareness. |
| What the example must do | Test a claim from Move 1 (for example, does a "transformational" turnaround rely on management systems as well?). |
| Alternative | A leadership failure with documented causes. It can show boundaries of a theory better than a success story. ⚠ choose one with reliable independent sources. |
| Must avoid | A paragraph of company history. A success story with no complication. |
| Sentence functions | 1. Name and context (one clause). 2. What it will test. 3. The sources you will triangulate. |

## 8.4 AC3 Move 1: Leader and rationale (85 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Name one leader from a diverse background and justify the choice with criteria. |
| Recommended | **Indra Nooyi** (former PepsiCo CEO). ⚠ verify all biographical facts. Why: documented leadership philosophy ("Performance with Purpose"); an intersectional story (woman, born in India, immigrant, leading a large multinational in a male-dominated setting); a record with both achievements and criticisms (for example, scrutiny of the health impact of the product portfolio). |
| Fallback | Satya Nadella (only if not used in AC1), or another leader meeting the criteria in section 10. |
| Rationale structure | State two or three **criteria**, show how the leader meets them, then say what you rejected and why. |
| Must avoid | "I admire her." A purely identity-based rationale. Reducing the leader to their demographic category. |
| Sentence functions | 1. Who and what kind of diversity. 2. Criteria (evidence quality, relevance to lens, analytical richness). 3. How they meet it. 4. Why not an obvious alternative. |
| Quality test | If the marker asked "why not [another famous leader]?", does your text already answer? |

## 8.5 AC3 Move 2: Theoretical lens (85 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | State the lens, how you will apply it, and where it falls short. |
| Recommended primary lens | **Intersectionality**: Crenshaw (1989), with Rosette et al. (2016) for leadership evaluation by race and gender. ⚠ Read both. Explains *how* overlapping identities shaped the leader's context and treatment. |
| Recommended secondary lens | **Transformational leadership** (Bass, 1985). Explains *what the leader did*. Connects directly to AC1. |
| Optional | **Role congruity** (Eagly and Karau, 2002): why behaviour that violates expectations may be penalised. |
| Operationalising | State what evidence you will examine (speeches, strategic decisions, stakeholder outcomes, critical commentary) and what *would count against* your interpretation. |
| Limits to name | Intersectionality is hard to apply to a single case and risks over-attribution. Evidence is retrospective and media-filtered. Transformational theory is leader-centred and can overstate individual agency. |
| Must avoid | Naming a lens without saying how it works on the case. Claiming the lens explains everything. |
| Sentence functions | 1. Lens and what it brings. 2. How you will use it on evidence. 3. One limit. 4. How you will compensate. |

## 8.6 Integration and risks (70 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Show the plan is one argument and identify the main risks. |
| Must contain | (a) A link between AC1 theory and AC3 analysis; (b) at least one named risk and mitigation. |
| Risk examples | Self-authored sources and "hero narrative" bias → triangulate with independent academic and press sources. Limited access to the leader's internal decisions → use published case analyses. Identity reductionism → analyse specific decisions and outcomes. |
| Must avoid | A summary of what you already said. Generic risks ("time management"). |

## 8.7 Literature engagement (50 words)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Job | Show you have read widely and noticed debates. |
| How | Do **not** create a separate "literature" paragraph unless needed. Spread citations through the plan, and use this budget for: noting a disagreement between sources; flagging a gap; distinguishing classic from recent work. |
| Evidence of engagement | Phrases such as "the evidence is contested", naming two authors who disagree, or stating which source is most credible and why. |

## 8.8 Task 2 cutting order (if over 500)

1. CUT FIRST: company background in the AC1 example.
2. CUT FIRST: the optional role congruity clause.
3. Shorten the orientation to one sentence.
4. Merge Literature engagement into the other moves.
5. Never cut: the critique in AC1, the criteria in AC3, the lens limitation, the risk.

# 9. AC1 theory selection: source-by-source notes

For each source: **what to take**, **what to criticise**, **where to use**. ⚠ Read the original before using any point below. These notes are memory-based summaries to direct your reading, not substitutes for it.

| Source | What to take | Critique or limit to consider | Use in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zaleznik (1977), *HBR* | Managers and leaders differ in orientation (stability and process versus vision and risk) | Strongly dichotomous; leaders as "special people" is contestable | AC1 Move 1 |
| Kotter (1990), *HBR* | Management copes with complexity (planning, organising, controlling); leadership copes with change (direction, alignment, motivation) | Practitioner argument; can be criticised for oversimplifying both roles | AC1 Move 1 |
| Mintzberg (1973), book | Empirical observation of managerial work; managers perform interpersonal, informational and decisional roles, including leadership | Dated sample; small-scale observation | AC1 Move 1, to challenge the dichotomy |
| Burns (1978), book | Transactional versus transforming leadership, rooted in political leadership | Conceptual, not easily measured | AC1 Move 1 and AC3 |
| Bass (1985), book | Develops transformational leadership (often summarised as four components) and measures it | Critiques include leader-centric focus and measurement overlap | AC1 and AC3 lens |
| Alvesson and Spicer (2012), *Human Relations* | Critical view of leadership studies; challenges taken-for-granted assumptions | Dense; use one specific point | AC1 critique |

# 12. Critical-thinking toolkit

These are **moves**, not sentences to copy. Each move shows the function the marker wants to see.

| Move | What it does | Where to use |
|---|---|---|
| Position then qualify | States a claim, then limits it precisely | Every paragraph |
| Contrast two authors | Shows you can see a debate | AC1 Move 1 |
| Apply then test | Uses theory on evidence, then says what it misses | Task 1 Move 2; AC3 |
| Source-bias flag | Notes that a source is self-interested | AC1 Move 2; AC3 |
| Counter-evidence acknowledgment | Names something that complicates your view | AC3; integration |
| Consequence for others | Moves from "I" to effect on people | Task 1 Move 3 |
| Trigger-and-change | Names what changed your mind and what changed | Task 1 Move 4 |
| Criterion-based choice | Justifies a selection by stated criteria | AC3 Move 1 |
| Operationalisation | States what evidence the lens will examine | AC3 Move 2 |
| Limit-of-method | States where your approach could mislead | Integration |

**Verbs that signal analysis (use accurately):** *challenges, qualifies, contrasts, exposes, overlooks, assumes, privileges, reconciles, tests, refines, undermines.*
**Verbs that signal description (avoid leaning on them):** *shows, explains, describes, discusses, looks at, talks about.*

**Calibrated certainty.** Use "suggests", "indicates", "is consistent with" for contested evidence. Use "demonstrates" only when the evidence is strong. Over-claiming is a common reason harsh markers withhold top grades.

# 13. Weak-versus-strong patterns (structure only)

These show the **shape** of weak and strong writing, with no wording to copy.

| Element | Weak pattern | Strong pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Theory use | Names a theory, then moves on | Names the theory, links it to one behaviour, states what it explains and misses |
| Self-evaluation | Lists traits | States a trait, evidence, effect on others, cause |
| Weakness | A strength in disguise | A gap with a real cost |
| Development plan | Vague intent ("improve communication") | Behaviour, setting, timeframe, success measure |
| Leader choice | "Because they inspire me" | Stated criteria, evidence quality, alternative rejected |
| Lens | Named only | Named, operationalised, limits stated |
| Sources | Single autobiography | Autobiography plus independent academic and press |
| Structure | Tour of ideas | One argument across sections |
| Evolution | "I learned a lot" | Before, trigger, after, changed behaviour |
| Referencing | Inconsistent, unused entries | Consistent, all matched |
