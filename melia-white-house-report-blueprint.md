# Meliá White House Report: Working Blueprint (Draft 1)

**Working title:** Delivering the Premium Promise: a consultancy look at customer experience and food & beverage at Meliá White House, London

**Length:** 3,000 words, excluding title page, contents, references and appendices.

---

## 0. The one-line argument

Meliá White House has put serious money (about £40m) into a premium repositioning, and the public spaces and breakfast deliver on it. The gaps are in the small, repeated touchpoints (lift, grilles, corridors, in-room coffee, breakfast disruption). Those gaps matter more at a £300 rate than at a £117 one. The fixes are cheap and quick.

Keep this sentence in mind for the intro and the conclusion. Every section should push it forward.

## 2. Aim and objectives

**Aim:** To judge how well Meliá White House's post-renovation strategy delivers a consistent premium guest experience, using food and beverage as the main lens, and to recommend practical improvements.

**Objectives**
1. Build a short theory toolkit from service quality, servicescape, expectation and strategic fit literature.
2. Examine the hotel's key decisions (repositioning, F&B concept, diversification).
3. Test how well those decisions work, using visit evidence and secondary sources together.
4. Recommend improvements that are costed, timed and measurable.

### Section 2: Approach and methods (300)

### Section 3: Theory toolkit (650)
Use **six** theories only. Roughly 100 words each. Each one: what it says, who disagrees, how you will use it.

| # | Theory | Who it's from | Who pushes back | How you use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gaps Model | Parasuraman et al. (1985) | It is provider-side and says little about the guest's feelings | Master framework. Your problems sit in Gap 3 (delivery vs standard) and Gap 4 (promise vs delivery) |
| 2 | SERVQUAL vs SERVPERF | Parasuraman et al. (1988); Cronin and Taylor (1992) | Cronin and Taylor: perceptions alone predict quality. Dimensions also shift between settings | You only have perceptions (reviews, observation), so take a SERVPERF-style approach and still use the five dimensions as a coding frame |
| 3 | Servicescape | Bitner (1992); Kotler (1973); heritage view from Timothy and Boyd (2003) | Built for controlled settings. Heritage wear can read as "authentic" or as "neglect" | Cornices and oak vs tarnished lift and dusty grille. At £300 a night, tolerance for wear drops |
| 4 | Expectation-disconfirmation | Oliver (1980) | Expectations are hard to measure and move over time | £300 quoted on the visit vs £117 on booking sites means guests arrive with very different expectations, which helps explain mixed reviews |
| 5 | Differentiation and strategic fit | Porter (1996), with Porter (1985) | "Stuck in the middle" critique; operational effectiveness is not strategy | A Mediterranean concept in public spaces but instant coffee in rooms is a fit failure, not just housekeeping |
| 6 | Service recovery | Hart et al. (1990); Tax et al. (1998) | Recovery can't replace reliability; fairness perceptions differ | Breakfast moved offsite after an electrical fault (per a review): was it handled well enough to turn a failure into goodwill? |

**Reserve list** (one sentence each at most, or leave out): experience economy (Pine and Gilmore, 1998) vs experiential marketing (Schmitt, 1999); customer journey (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016); service-profit chain (Heskett et al., 1994; Ford et al., 2012); F&B quality (Namkung and Jang, 2007; Ryu et al., 2012; Johns and Pine, 2002); yield (Kimes, 1989); green consumption (Han et al., 2010); reviews (Xiang et al., 2015; Filieri et al., 2015; Ye et al., 2009).

> Marker's view: six argued theories beat thirteen name-drops. The reserve list is there so you can pull one in where it directly helps (e.g. Han et al. in the sustainability decision, Kimes in the pricing recommendation) without bloating Section 3.

### Section 4: Four decisions (850, roughly 210 each)
Write each paragraph as **claim, theory, evidence, judgement**. The judgement sentence is where marks are earned.

**Decision 1: £40m, five-year repositioning while open (LO1)**
- Theory: Barney (1991), the listed building as a rare, valuable resource. Teece et al. (1997), renovating while trading as a dynamic capability.
- Evidence: Business Traveller (2024) for the figures. Reviews that mention faults or disruption.
- Judgement angle: the heritage asset is a real advantage, but trading through works carried a cost in disruption, and it shows in reviews.

**Decision 2: Mediterranean F&B concept**
- What: Arado, buffet, Spanish wines and cava.
- Theory: Porter (1996) on activity fit. Kotler et al. on positioning.
- Evidence: Figs. 2 and 3 (buffet counter with GMED oils and Real Crisps; Codorníu organic cava on ice). Breakfast is the most praised item in reviews.
- Judgement angle: the concept is distinctive and consistent where guests can see it, and breaks down where they can't (in-room coffee, Fig. 5).

**Decision 3: Diversification, The Level and public co-working (LO1)**
- What: ten workspaces from £40 a day, 10% off at Arado and 35 Bar (Business Traveller, 2024).
- Theory: Enz (2010) on strategy and new revenue. This is enterprise behaviour: a new income stream from underused space, plus cross-selling.
- Evidence: Fig. 4 (lounge and communal table), Fig. 8 ('35 Bar).
- Judgement angle: smart use of space, but you don't know the usage numbers, so say that and don't overclaim.

**Decision 4: Group premium strategy and sustainability**
- What: premium and luxury are about 65% of Meliá's portfolio; the group has a sustainability record.
- Theory: Han et al. (2010) on whether guests actually pay for green credentials (the attitude-behaviour gap).
- Evidence: Brand Finance coverage; organic cava on the visit.
- Judgement angle: these are corporate claims from promotional sources. Treat sustainability as supporting value, not the lead.

### Section 5: How well is it working? (450)
- **Triangulation** of the visit, staff dialogue, planning/press documents and reviews. Saunders et al. (2019). Honest note: three review platforms reduce single-platform bias but not self-selection bias.
- **Review findings:** quote totals from Table D and show the bar chart in an appendix.
- **Gap analysis:** quote the two or three most important rows of Table E. Link each to a Gap 3 or Gap 4 reading.
- **The price angle:** £300 vs £117 (Oliver). Same worn lift is "character" at one price and "neglect" at the other (Bitner, Timothy and Boyd).
- **Staff:** reviews that name individual staff are a clear positive. Mention the service-profit chain only briefly, and note its causal links are weakly supported.

## 5. Writing style (so it reads like analysis, not description)
- **Compare authors in the same paragraph.** Example: "Parasuraman et al. (1988) argue X, whereas Cronin and Taylor (1992) contend Y; this report takes Z because the available data are perceptions-based."
- **Paragraph pattern:** claim, theory, evidence (Fig. or source), judgement.
- **Use consultancy words:** diagnosis, strategic fit, touchpoint, service recovery, positioning, yield, value proposition, brand consistency.
- **Don't describe the hotel.** If a sentence doesn't argue or evaluate, cut it.
- **Photos:** cite as Fig. n in the text, place them in Appendix 1 with captions.
- **Weakest-link idea:** guests remember the weakest touchpoint, not the best one (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). Use it once in Section 5 or 6.
