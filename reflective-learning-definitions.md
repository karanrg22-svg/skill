# Reflective Learning: Two Clashing Definitions

## 1. Definition (150 words)

Kolb (1984) treats reflective learning as a cycle. You have a concrete experience, reflect on it, pull an abstract idea out of that reflection, then test the idea in practice. That test hands you a new experience and the loop starts again. Reflection is one stage in that engine; skip a stage and the learning stalls.

Moon (1999) drops the stages. For her, reflective learning happens when a new experience gets worked into your existing cognitive structures, your assumptions and prior understanding, and comes out the other side as a changed or better-integrated meaning. There's no required order and no fixed number of steps. Reflection can start anywhere and run at different depths, from a shallow description of what happened to a genuine rethink of what you believed.

Kolb defines reflection by its shape. Moon defines it by what it does to you.

## 2. Kolb (1984) in my own words

Kolb builds a closed loop with a fixed shape. Something happens to you: concrete experience. You notice what happened and how: reflective observation. You pull a rule or principle out of it: abstract conceptualisation. You go test that rule in a new situation, active experimentation, which gives you your next experience, and the cycle restarts. Reflection is the hinge between raw experience and abstract theory, not the whole of learning. Kolb's claim is basically procedural: work through the stages in roughly this order and learning follows. The process is the theory.

## 3. Moon (1999) instead

Moon cares less about which stages you pass through and more about what changes in your head. Reflective learning, for her, is taking a messy, unstructured experience and working it into what you already believe. You compare it against your existing framework, notice where it doesn't fit, then either adjust the new material to fit or reorganise the framework itself. No named sequence. Reflection can be triggered at any point and can be shallow or deep. What matters is whether your understanding was restructured, or whether you just described the experience back to yourself. The outcome is the theory.

## 4. Where they actually disagree

The fault line is process versus outcome. Kolb says reflection happened because you completed the cycle. Moon says it happened because your understanding changed, however you got there. That bites in practice. Someone can grind through all four Kolb stages and produce nothing but a restatement of events: box ticked, nothing learned. By Moon's standard, that person never reflected at all, even though their process looked correct. Flip it around and someone can jump straight from an experience to a changed belief with no orderly stages at all. Moon counts that without hesitation. Kolb has nowhere to put it. The two theories disagree about what counts as evidence: finishing a procedure, or showing that meaning shifted.

## 5. Which fits me better

Moon's version. When I actually reflect on something, I don't march through four labeled stages in order. It's messier than that. Sometimes I notice the mismatch before I've even finished the experience; sometimes it hits me days later out of nowhere. What tells me reflection happened isn't that I completed a cycle, it's that I catch myself thinking about the thing differently than I did before.

Kolb falls short because his model can't tell the difference between real reflection and going through the motions. You can sit down, dutifully narrate what happened, generalise a lesson from it, and never actually change your mind about anything. I've done that: written a tidy after-action summary that hit every stage and taught me nothing, because I was performing the process rather than letting the experience unsettle what I already believed. Moon's test catches that. If my understanding didn't shift, I didn't reflect, no matter how many stages I ticked off.
