# Reflective Learning: Two Clashing Definitions

## 1. Definition (150 words)

Reflective learning can be defined in two competing ways. **Kolb (1984)** frames it as a **cyclical process**: learning happens by moving through four stages — concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, and active experimentation — and then looping back into a new experience. Reflection here is one *stage* in a repeatable engine that converts doing into knowing, and its value lies in the movement itself: without cycling through all four stages, learning stalls.

**Moon (1999)**, by contrast, treats reflection as a **cognitive restructuring process** rather than a sequence of stages. For Moon, reflective learning happens when new experience is worked into existing "cognitive structures" (schemas, assumptions, prior understanding), producing a *changed* or more integrated meaning. The mechanism isn't a loop with fixed steps — it's the reworking of mental structure itself, however that reworking happens to occur.

## 2. Kolb (1984) in my own words

Kolb's reflective learning is a **process model**: a closed loop with a defined shape. You have a concrete experience (something happens to you), you reflect on it (reflective observation — noticing what happened and how), you generalise from it (abstract conceptualisation — forming a rule, theory, or principle), and then you test that generalisation in a new situation (active experimentation), which produces a fresh experience and restarts the cycle. Reflection isn't the whole of learning — it's the hinge between raw experience and abstract theory. The implicit claim is procedural: if you go through the stages, in something like this order, learning will occur. The *process* is the theory.

## 3. Moon (1999) instead

Moon is less interested in what stages you pass through and more interested in **what changes inside your head** as a result. For her, reflective learning is the process of taking a "messy," unstructured experience and working it into existing cognitive structures — comparing it against what you already believe, noticing misfit, and adjusting your framework so the new material fits (or reorganising the framework itself if it doesn't). There's no required sequence of named stages; reflection can be triggered at any point and can operate at different depths (from surface description to genuine reconceptualisation). What matters is the **outcome**: has your understanding actually been restructured, or have you just described the experience back to yourself? The *outcome* is the theory.

## 4. Where they actually disagree

The sharpest tension is exactly the **process vs. outcome** split: Kolb defines reflective learning by its *shape* (a repeatable four-stage loop — you know you've reflected because you completed the cycle), while Moon defines it by its *effect* (a change in cognitive structure — you know you've reflected because your understanding is now different, however you got there). This has a real practical consequence: under Kolb, someone could dutifully move through all four stages and produce nothing but a superficial restatement of events — the loop completed, box ticked, but nothing learned. Under Moon, that same person would not have "reflected" at all, because no restructuring occurred, even though the process looked correct. Conversely, someone could restructure their understanding through a messy, non-sequential process — jumping straight from experience to a changed belief — which Moon would happily count as reflective learning, but which wouldn't map cleanly onto Kolb's ordered stages. So the disagreement isn't cosmetic: it changes what you'd point to as *evidence* that reflection actually happened — completion of a procedure versus a demonstrable shift in meaning.
