# 2. Experience or Case Analysis

## 2.1 Case introduction, relevance and analytical approach

This portfolio reflects on one episode from a formal, several-week college placement on an NHS hospital inpatient ward. The duration and defined task make it substantial, and it is a reflection on my own learning, not data collection. My task was to support documentation using the output of an AI-supported imaging tool. One flag appeared inconsistent, although I was unable to say exactly why. I told my supervisor, saw no clear follow-up, and do not know the outcome. Details are altered and anonymised, and my college contact and the placement provider gave written permission.

AI in diagnostics is live NHS policy. NHSX (2019) set out how to adopt it safely, and the Department of Health and Social Care (2025) anticipates wider use. Yet no statute regulates healthcare AI as such: software with a medical purpose is regulated as a medical device (Medical Devices Regulations 2002; Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency [MHRA], 2023), while safe local use falls to clinical safety standards and provider governance (NHS England, 2018). Whether this combination protects patients adequately is contestable. The case matters because it sits where policy ambition meets the least experienced person on the ward.

I analyse three issues that follow an uncertain output from tool to record to person: accuracy, safety and accountability (2.2); communication, escalation and speaking up (2.3); and records integrity, transparency and confidentiality (2.4). The lenses are a systems view of safety (Reason, 2000), the automation bias literature, psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) and, for my own judgement, tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1966). I use Gibbs (1988) as a spine, offsetting its descriptive tendency as explained in 1.4. The reflection has limits that I treat as part of the analysis. I discuss the law of England, separate binding law from guidance, and offer legal statements as my reading of the cited sources, not advice. I grade sources by what they can show: statute and regulations bind, guidance advises, and studies vary in setting and design. I describe only what I personally saw and did, I do not claim the tool was wrong or non-compliant, and I do not evaluate the service or colleagues.

## 2.2 Issue 1: Accuracy, safety and accountability

**What I did.** I used the tool's output to help complete documentation. A flag appeared inconsistent; I could not say why, only that it seemed off. I knew what the tool displayed. I did not know how it had been validated, what population it reflected, or whether the ward had checked it locally. I told my supervisor. **The case for the tool.** A fair account starts with what such tools offer: consistent attention to every image, flags that busy clinicians might otherwise miss, and time released for patient contact (Topol, 2019). I do not dispute this. My point is narrower: benefit depends on how output is used, because an over-trusted assistive tool adds a route to error while appearing to remove one.

**Why uncertain output is hazardous.** Three mechanisms matter. First, automation bias: people over-rely on automated advice, missing errors the tool misses and following errors it makes (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010). Goddard et al. (2012) reviewed evidence that clinicians can follow incorrect advice, with mixed evidence on mitigations such as training. In mammography, Dratsch et al. (2023) found that incorrect AI suggestions lowered reader accuracy, especially for less experienced readers. Second, anchoring: an early value shapes later judgement (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974), and a flag displayed first is such a value. Third, performance does not travel automatically. Seyyed-Kalantari et al. (2021) found that chest radiograph algorithms underdiagnosed some under-served groups, so published accuracy may not describe a particular ward. These studies concern radiology readers or retrospective datasets, not students documenting on a ward, so they support caution, not prediction. In my situation the relevant risk was error propagation: a flag copied into a record may later be read as a clinician's finding. Anchoring meant a flag could shape what I wrote even if I doubted it, and automation bias meant that a student, deferring to both the tool and the hierarchy, may be the person least likely to question it.

**"Just a feeling".** I could not explain my unease. Polanyi (1966) argued that we know more than we can tell, Benner (1984) links such perceptual sensitivity to expertise, which a novice has little of, and Schön (1983) describes its in-the-moment form as reflection-in-action. A fair objection is that intuition is also where bias hides (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974), so a feeling cannot count as evidence against a tool. I accept this, and it is the point: intuition is a legitimate safety signal but unreliable evidence, so it justifies a check, not a conclusion. Saying the flag "appeared inconsistent", not that it was wrong, is therefore a matter of accuracy about what I knew.

**What the framework requires.** Diagnostic software is normally regulated as a medical device under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002 (as amended), with guidance from the MHRA (2023). The clinical safety standards DCB0129 (manufacturers) and DCB0160 (deploying organisations) require clinical risk management (NHS England, 2018). Providers must deliver safe care and treatment and operate good governance under Regulations 12 and 17 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014. I do not know how the tool or the ward met these requirements and do not suggest they were unmet. My critical point is structural: this regime approves a product and requires local risk management, but safe use still depends on staff who understand the tool's limits, and nothing I know of requires that a placement student is told them. Regulation sits upstream of the moment I met the tool; the moment itself was governed by local practice.

**Accountability.** Decisions on care remain with registered professionals, whose regulators expect independent judgement (Health and Care Professions Council, 2016; Nursing and Midwifery Council, 2018). Matthias (2004) describes a "responsibility gap" where learning systems behave in ways nobody fully controls; on a ward the gap is practical, because a usually accurate tool invites deference. As a student I was not the reporting clinician and could not verify an image. "Never accept AI output unchecked" was my first conclusion, but it overstates what I could do. Checking should be proportionate and done by the right person: mine was to notice, question, escalate and avoid recording uncertain output as fact; a registered clinician's extends to independent verification. Asking a question was within a student's remit, because a question claims no authority and leaves the decision with the person who holds it. Employers carry duties too: Regulation 17 places governance responsibilities on providers, and the duty of candour (Regulation 20) operates at organisational level (Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014). Individual accountability without system support would be unfair and unsafe.

**So what.** Two implications go forward. A route for concerns that returns an outcome, and a convention marking AI-derived entries as unverified until confirmed. Both depend on induction that tells students what the tool cannot do.

## 2.3 Issue 2: Communication, escalation and speaking up

**What I did, and what it took.** I told my supervisor. Speaking up as a student is harder than the policy suggests. Francis (2015) found that NHS staff often felt unable to raise concerns, and the NHS Constitution (Department of Health and Social Care, 2023) affirms a right to do so. Power gradients and uncertainty work against a junior with only a feeling to report (Francis, 2015). Edmondson (1999) defines psychological safety as a shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe, and argues that it enables learning in teams. In hierarchical settings this belief is fragile: a student who is wrong risks embarrassment, and one who is right risks appearing to challenge a senior colleague's reliance on the tool. **What a good message holds.** SBAR gives structure: situation, background, assessment, recommendation (Haig et al., 2006). Applied here, it would have turned a feeling into a message: the flag appeared inconsistent; I was completing documentation; I could not say why; please check. A systematic review found the evidence for SBAR to be mostly low-quality studies of hospital settings (Müller et al., 2018), so I treat it as a useful prompt, not proof of safer care. Nancarrow et al. (2013) describe good interdisciplinary teamwork through clear roles, communication and mutual respect, and Reeves et al. (2017) found the evidence on interprofessional collaboration limited and varied. Transfer from those studies to a student querying an AI output is my inference, not a finding.

**Who sets the norm.** Whether a student's doubt is welcomed is decided by those with authority. West (2021) describes compassionate leadership as attending to, understanding and helping colleagues; applied here, supervisors would signal in advance that querying an AI output is expected, not merely tolerated. The Healthcare Leadership Model (NHS Leadership Academy, 2013) treats such behaviours as relevant at every level. In the sources I read, leadership writing is largely normative and offers little evidence of effect on specific safety outcomes, so I treat it as a design principle, not a demonstrated remedy. For me it implies a future responsibility: to say at induction that questioning the tool is part of the job.

**No visible follow-up.** I do not know what happened. Staff may have checked and found nothing amiss, dealt with it without telling a student, or not pursued it; I cannot distinguish these, and confidentiality may legitimately limit what students are told. The absence of visible follow-up is not evidence of inaction. Its effect on the person who spoke up is the same, though. Berwick (2013) argues that a safe system learns from concerns, and Reason (2000) places organisational learning at the centre of safety. A concern with no visible outcome teaches that raising concerns achieves nothing, discouraging the next one and wasting the information; Francis (2015) reported that staff who spoke up often saw no action. A closed loop would acknowledge the concern, say who looked at it, and share whatever outcome can be shared without identifying anyone.

**Candid evaluation.** I escalated under uncertainty, within my remit, without claiming knowledge I lacked. I could have done more. I did not ask how the tool was validated or when it should not be used. I did not ask afterwards what had been found. [YOU SUPPLY: confirm whether you wrote anything down; if not, say so plainly.] A short concern note, stating what I saw, why I was uncertain, whom I told and when, would have created a contemporaneous record and a basis for follow-up. Communication and safety interact: weak escalation lets automation bias persist unchallenged, and a culture in which concerns vanish makes the next student less likely to raise one.

## 2.4 Issue 3: Records integrity, transparency and confidentiality

**Accuracy.** UK GDPR Article 5(1)(d), applied through the Data Protection Act 2018, requires personal data to be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date. Regulation 17(2)(c) requires accurate, complete and contemporaneous records (Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014). An AI flag recorded without qualification asserts something the service may not have verified, and later clinicians may read it as established. On my reading, this raises a question under the accuracy principle that depends on how an entry is worded and verified, which I cannot assess for the ward. The NHS Records Management Code of Practice (NHS England, 2023) governs retention and handling, not how to word a record of uncertain AI output.

Error propagation has a particular mechanism in records. Once an entry exists, later readers treat it as the product of professional judgement, because that is why records are trusted, and its AI origin may not be visible to them. Reason's (2000) systems view locates the hazard in the pathway, where one uncertain output can pass through several hands without anyone owning its accuracy, not in any one person.

**Transparency and confidentiality.** For direct care, the lawful basis under UK GDPR is usually public task (Article 6(1)(e)) with Article 9(2)(h) for health data, not consent (Information Commissioner's Office, no date). Telling a patient that AI supports their imaging is therefore a transparency duty (UK GDPR, Articles 12 to 14), not a request for permission, which differs conceptually from consent to treatment. The common-law duty of confidentiality and the Caldicott Principles, including that people should be informed about how their information is used (National Data Guardian, 2020), point the same way. Article 22 concerns decisions based solely on automated processing, and reforms in the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 reshaped it, so the current text needs checking; where a clinician reviews the output, it is unlikely to be engaged on my reading. I do not know whether patients on the ward were told about the tool and will not guess.

**Evaluation.** The principles are clear, but practical instruction does not reach the person writing the entry. Legislation and codes address controllers and registered professionals, and I am not aware of guidance telling a student how to word an AI-derived entry or record a concern without identifiers. Transparency can also become a notice that exists without being understood; its adequacy is better judged by patient understanding than by compliance. Meaningful transparency would explain, in plain language and at a time a patient can use it, that software supports the reading of images, that a clinician reviews the output, and whom to ask. I know of no evidence on whether notices of this kind change what patients understand in this setting, so I treat adequacy as an open question. A counter-position holds that labelling AI-derived entries would clutter records and slow clinicians. That cost is real, and S2 addresses it by limiting the label to entries that influence decisions and lifting it on confirmation. The same discipline applies to this portfolio: it contains no record content, no identifiers and no product details, which is itself a confidentiality decision.

## 2.5 Integrated evaluation and solutions

**Verdict.** Three tensions run through the issues: efficiency against verification; transparency against data minimisation, since a concern note must say enough to be useful without identifying anyone; and individual against system accountability. The verdict is conditional because the evidence neither shows these tools to be unsafe nor shows safeguards to be unnecessary; benefit and harm are both plausible, and the difference lies in local conditions. My verdict is a conditional endorsement: AI-supported imaging output is defensible in documentation only where entries are marked as unverified until confirmed, concerns return an outcome, and staff and students are told the tool's limits.

| Solution | Evidence | Feasibility | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 Concern-and-closure routine: a short note (what I saw, why uncertain, whom I told, when) and a named feedback route | Berwick (2013); Francis (2015); Edmondson (1999) | High: low-cost process change | Could become ritual; confidentiality limits feedback; workload |
| S2 "AI-suggested, unverified" label until a registered clinician confirms | Parasuraman and Manzey (2010); UK GDPR Art. 5(1)(d) | Medium: needs local policy | Clutter and label fatigue |
| S3 Induction on AI tools for all staff and students | Goddard et al. (2012); NHS England (2018) | Medium | Training decays; effect uncertain |

I rank S1 first. It is cheap, directly repairs the broken feedback loop, and is my original proposal, offered for a service like the one I worked in, not as criticism of it. As a small test it could run on one ward for eight weeks as a plan-do-study-act cycle (Langley et al., 2009), measuring the share of concerns acknowledged within a set period and staff and student confidence to speak up, with a review point at the end. S1's main weakness is that it can be completed without being used, and counting notes written would reward volume, so the pilot should also ask staff whether a concern changed what happened next. S3 follows because induction supports the other two; it should include what to do when a tool seems wrong, since training that explains only benefits could increase deference. S2 comes last because it needs governance agreement, and its benefit rests on my inference from the automation bias literature, not on direct evidence.

**Limitations.** This is one episode, written from memory, without empirical data. I do not know the outcome, and my knowledge of the tool is limited to what I saw. The findings are analytic and not generalisable.

## 2.6 Learning, Gibbs action and conclusion

The evidence changed that: a second look is independent only if the human forms a view first; otherwise the flag anchors them (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974; Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010). I also believed that verifying meant checking the output. I now see it as noticing, questioning and escalating in proportion to my role.

My action plan is behavioural. When asked to record AI-derived information, I will label it as AI-suggested and awaiting clinician confirmation, consistent with the accuracy principle and Regulation 17. When uncertain, I will tell my supervisor within the same shift using SBAR, and write a concern note without identifiers, as local policy allows. At the start of any placement using AI tools, I will ask how the tool was validated and when it should not be used. A week after raising a concern, I will ask whether it has been closed, accepting that I may not be told the outcome.

These behaviours help only if the people around me can sustain them. That requires knowledge of governance, quality improvement and leadership, which shapes Section 3. The learning also has limits: one episode cannot show how I would act in a different team, or when my doubt is stronger and the person who can decide is harder to reach. I will test the plan through supervisor feedback in my next role.
