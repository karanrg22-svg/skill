# 1. Professional Identity

## 1.1 Position and orientation

I am becoming a practitioner who treats judgement as something a service must protect, not something an individual simply possesses. My route runs from a PCL nursing diploma in Nepal (2023) and hospital practice at BP Smriti, through care-home work in the United Kingdom (UK), to my current degree. Each setting taught the same lesson from a different direction: the quality of care follows the conditions in which people are allowed to think, question and speak. By independent judgement I mean a decision I can explain, defend and revise, not a decision made alone.

This portfolio tests that claim. Section 1 traces where it comes from. Section 2 examines it through one placement episode in which an AI-supported imaging tool's flag appeared inconsistent and I could not say exactly why. Section 3 sets out a career plan built from what that analysis showed I still need to develop.

## 1.2 Learning that changed my thinking

**Health and access are structurally produced.** I once believed unequal access to care was mainly a shortage of staff and equipment. Dahlgren and Whitehead's (1991) layered model, the World Health Organization Commission on Social Determinants of Health (2008) and Marmot et al. (2020) argue instead that income, housing and power shape who reaches care. This reframed what I had seen of rural access in Nepal, although the model explains care-home inequalities less well, where staffing patterns and supervision appear to matter as much as social position. I now ask what in the system produces a difference before attributing it to individuals, because a barrier I read as a person's choice, such as a missed appointment, may be one the service built. **Accountability is individual and systemic.** I used to equate accountability with following procedure and answering for my own errors. Francis (2013) and Berwick (2013) showed that serious failures grew from cultures and systems, and Reason (2000) contrasts person and system approaches. Yet the Mental Capacity Act 2005 (s.1) still places personal duties on practitioners, such as presuming capacity, so systems thinking cannot dissolve individual responsibility. I therefore ask why a rule exists and where it fails, then follow it or challenge it through the proper route. This avoids two errors: blaming individuals for failures that systems made likely, and excusing individuals by pointing to systems. [YOU SUPPLY: one example from your work, 20 words.]

**Care is delivered by teams and systems.** I thought good care came from being a good carer. Nancarrow et al. (2013) identify team conditions, such as clear roles and communication, that no individual can supply alone, and West (2021) argues that compassionate leadership sustains them. The evidence is largely descriptive and hospital-based (Reeves et al., 2017), so transfer to social care is uncertain. My practice has shifted towards shaping how handovers and reporting work, not only performing my own tasks well. Influence over how a team works does not wait for a title; it begins with how I hand over, report and respond when a colleague raises a doubt. ## 1.3 Values, attitudes and standards

Dignity and choice often conflict with safety. I hold all three, so the useful question is how they are weighed. [YOU SUPPLY: one anonymised care-home incident, two sentences, my own learning only, no identifiable detail.]

The Mental Capacity Act 2005 (ss.1, 4) offers a structure: capacity is presumed, an unwise decision does not show incapacity, and decisions for a person lacking capacity follow their best interests. The Care Act 2014 (s.1) makes wellbeing the guiding principle, while Regulations 10 and 12 of the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 (regs. 10, 12) require dignity and safe care together. Beauchamp and Childress (2019) supply the vocabulary, but principles do not rank themselves, and the Skills for Care (2013) Code of Conduct states what workers must do without telling them how to weigh a conflict.

Nepal and the UK show different costs. UK documentation and safeguarding systems create traceability and protection, but can consume time and, where fear of blame dominates, encourage defensive practice (Berwick, 2013). In the Nepali hospital settings I knew, resource limits meant that judgement and relationships carried more of the load, and protection depended more on individual conscience (Ministry of Health and Population, 2023). Neither is superior; each protects different things. Both generalisations come from limited settings, so I hold them as questions to test, not as findings about either country.

The standard I now hold myself to: when dignity and safety conflict, I record my reasoning and the person's own view, and consult a senior colleague before restricting a choice.

## 1.4 Reflective method and competency documentation

Gibbs (1988) is structured and accessible, but is criticised as descriptive and weak on critique (Finlay, 2008). Schön (1983) captures reflection-in-action, which is hard to evidence in writing (Finlay, 2008). Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper (2001) are simple but can stay shallow. I use Gibbs as a spine and compensate by pairing each stage with evidence and an explicit judgement, borrowing Schön for moments of intuition (2.2). Evidence-based practice has similar limits: Sackett et al. (1996) rely on a hierarchy of evidence that Greenhalgh, Howick and Maskrey (2014) argue fits poorly with complex, person-centred care, so I grade each source by what it can show. I map my competencies to the Care Certificate standards (Skills for Care, Health Education England and Skills for Health, 2015) and the Healthcare Leadership Model (NHS Leadership Academy, 2013), remembering that a record of attendance is not competence (3.2).

## 1.5 Synthesis and bridge

I now claim that I act as a practitioner who notices, questions and escalates, and who records the reasoning so that others can learn from it. That claim can be tested against three observable behaviours: what I did when unsure, what I recorded, and whether I learned what happened next. The case in Section 2 asks whether, under uncertainty and with limited status, I did so, and where my practice fell short.
