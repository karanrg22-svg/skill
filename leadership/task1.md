In Goleman's (1998) terms, strong social skill without self-awareness means I influence people without checking how I come across. My default style resembled the affiliative one, which Goleman (2000) warns can avoid difficult conversations. It did not fit: the group needed challenge, not harmony, and my instinct is to smooth over disagreement instead of surfacing it. I also delegated tasks but kept the final say, which probably left teammates little room to speak. This framing has limits. Antonakis, Ashkanasy and Dasborough (2009) argue that emotional intelligence is poorly defined and measured, so my self-rating may depend on the model used.

My strength is real: teammates relied on my competence [YOU SUPPLY: one piece of evidence]. The gap cost something. A teammate carried a problem alone, and trust built on ability more than benevolence (Mayer, Davis and Schoorman, 1995) gave them less reason to tell me.

I once believed leadership was a natural trait. Activity 1 and the workbook changed that: self-awareness can be developed, and noticing others achieves little unless I act on it. Empathy, I now see, is information for better decisions, not simply being kind.

My potential is credible but unproven, provided I seek the feedback I currently lack. Success means a teammate raising a concern with me unprompted. I will develop this into my AC4 plan.
