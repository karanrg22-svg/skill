## 1. Introduction

Meliá White House is a Grade II listed 1930s building on Albany Street, London, operated by Meliá since 1999. Meliá reports a £40m, five-year renovation completed while the hotel kept trading (Hospitality Net, 2023), and Business Traveller (2024) dates completion to April 2023. The hotel now sells itself as premium.

The question is whether the hotel keeps that promise at every point a guest touches, and if not, what is the cheapest way to close the gap. The aim is to assess how consistently the post-renovation strategy reaches the guest, using food and beverage (F&B) as the main lens, and to recommend practical, costed fixes. Four objectives follow:

1. Which lenses from the literature suit the problem? (Chapter 3)
2. Were four management decisions good enterprise choices? (Chapter 4)
3. How well do those decisions work, judged by visit evidence, documents and a coded review sample? (Chapter 5)
4. What four fixes are costed, timed and measurable? (Chapter 6)

The argument is that Meliá White House delivers its premium promise where guests look first (public spaces, breakfast, staff) and under-delivers where they look closely (condition and consistency), which matters more at a £300 room rate than at £117. Chapter 2 sets out the method, Chapters 3 and 4 the tools and decisions, Chapter 5 the evidence, and Chapters 6 and 7 the recommendations and conclusion.

## 2. Approach and methods

The work follows five consulting stages: entry, diagnosis, data gathering, feedback and recommendations. Greiner and Metzger (1983) supply the staged logic, and Cope (2003) stresses that a consultant's credibility and communication decide whether advice is used. A student consultant lacks both a contract and a client feedback loop, so entry and feedback are simulated, and the report claims diagnosis and recommendation only. This is an appraisal of the process, not a defence of it: the weakest stage is feedback, since no client has tested the findings.

The design is a single-case study (Yin, 2018). One hotel gives depth over breadth, which suits a question about consistency across touchpoints, but it limits generalisation.

Primary data came from non-participant observation and photographs on a hosted tour on 9 September 2025 [CONFIRM AGAINST YOUR NOTES] (Veal, 2017), and from an informal staff session. That session focused on careers and the tour was hosted, so the visit was not neutral: it showed a managed version of the hotel. Staff remarks are paraphrased, not treated as policy.

Secondary data comprised Camden Council planning documents, Meliá press material, trade press, Brand Finance and S&P coverage, and a coded sample of online reviews analysed thematically (Braun and Clarke, 2006). Following Denzin (1978) and Bryman and Bell (2015), each claim is checked against at least two source types, because no single source here is free of bias.

The limits are specific. There was one visit and one managed tour. Management accounts were unavailable. Corporate sources are promotional, and the review sample of 36 [CONFIRM FINAL NUMBER] is self-selected and indicative only (Litvin, Goldsmith and Pan, 2008). Sources also conflict: Meliá's press material gives 581 bedrooms, while a Camden Council document gives 548 (Camden Council, 2025). Both are reported and neither is chosen silently. No identifiable staff or guests appear in any photograph, and reviews are paraphrased and cited by platform and month only. Appendix 2 maps each source.

## 3. Six lenses

Six lenses are used because each reappears in the decisions and recommendations that follow.

**Technical and functional quality.** Grönroos (1984) separates what is delivered (the room, the food) from how it is delivered (manner and responsiveness). Pizam and Ellis (1999) counter that hospitality satisfaction is multi-attribute in practice, so the two sides blur. Read together, they suggest the hotel's strength lies in process and its weakness in outcome details. The distinction matters for a consultant because the two sides are fixed by different teams: process by training and supervision, outcomes by engineering and housekeeping standards. This lens sorts the evidence.

**Must-be, performance and delight.** Kano et al. (1984) hold that some features are expected and invisible when present, while others delight. Categories are normally set by survey and shift by guest type, so here they are inferred from reviews and photographs, and the report says so. A clean lift and grille are must-be, while Arado and Spanish cava are attractive. Adding delight on top of cracked basics wastes money. The framework also warns that today's delight becomes tomorrow's expectation, so Arado's success raises the bar for the rooms.

**The setting.** Turley and Milliman (2000) treat atmosphere as a driver of behaviour, but their evidence comes largely from retail. Poria, Butler and Airey (2003) suggest heritage visitors value a personal link with the past, so worn fabric can read as character. The same patina can equally read as neglect once the guest has paid a premium rate. The lens tests cornices and oak against a tarnished lift door and a dusty grille. A fair reading must also allow that guests differ: those who chose the hotel for its heritage may forgive what others notice at once.

**Value and fairness.** Zeithaml (1988) places value in the guest's head, so it differs between guests. Xia, Monroe and Cox (2004) add that price differences are judged for fairness, while Kimes (2002) notes that yield pricing is rational for the hotel yet can feel unfair. Rooms quoted at about £300 on the visit and about £117 on booking sites [VERIFY BOTH] create guests with very different yardsticks. The same lift door can therefore be forgiven at one price and resented at another, which helps explain mixed reviews of the same building.

**Positioning.** Kim and Mauborgne (2004) reward standing out by raising what guests value, whereas Treacy and Wiersema (1993) warn that a firm should excel at one value discipline. Blue-ocean claims also rest on selected success cases. The lens asks whether a premium, heritage and Spanish-food story is too ambitious to hold across every touchpoint. Both authors agree that a position needs coherence, which is the test applied below.

**Service recovery.** Smith, Bolton and Wagner (1999) show that how a failure is put right shapes later satisfaction, yet McCollough, Berry and Yadav (2000) caution that recovery cannot substitute for reliability. The lens is applied to the breakfast disruption. Because the evidence is a single review, it is used cautiously, to shape a protocol rather than to judge past performance.

Together the lenses form a chain: Grönroos classifies what was seen, Kano ranks its importance, the setting and value lenses explain why it matters at this price, positioning asks whether the strategy can hold, and recovery asks how failure is handled.

## 4. Four decisions analysed

**Decision 1: the big bet.** Meliá reports £40m invested over five years while the hotel stayed open (Hospitality Net, 2023). Grant (1991) would treat a Grade II listed building as a scarce resource that rivals cannot copy. Eisenhardt and Martin (2000) frame renovating while trading as a capability, because revenue and learning continue. Poria et al. (2003) question whether guests actually value heritage, and the lift door and grille (Figs. 1 and 6) show how quickly heritage becomes a liability if it is not maintained. Reviews that mention faults or disruption [INSERT COUNT FROM APPENDIX 4] suggest guests absorbed part of the cost, and Camden filings show works continuing in 2025, including a Wing A refurbishment and a basement conversion (Camden Council, 2025) [CHECK]. The verdict is that the asset justified the bet, but trading through works moved the cost onto guests, and a £40m spend only protects value if standards are then sustained. Put simply, the building is the hotel's moat only for as long as it looks cared for, and the renovation creates an obligation to maintain it, not merely to reach a standard once.

**Decision 2: the Spanish signature.** Arado, the buffet, Spanish wines and organic cava give the hotel a distinct food and drink identity. Kim and Mauborgne (2004) would praise the distinctiveness, but Treacy and Wiersema (1993) would ask whether it is carried through everywhere. The buffet counter with GMED oils and Real Crisps and the organic cava on ice (Figs. 2 and 3) show the concept working where guests can see it. Buffet operations depend on consistent replenishment and presentation (Davis et al., 2018) [CHECK PAGE], which the lit shelving suggests was achieved on the visit. Breakfast is the most praised item in the review sample [INSERT COUNT]. The concept breaks in the room, where instant coffee (Fig. 5) contradicts the Spanish, premium identity. The verdict is that the signature is a good bet, undermined by a fixable inconsistency, and Gentile, Spiller and Noci (2007) explain why it matters: experience is built from many components, so a weak one dilutes the rest. A guest who enjoys Spanish oils and cava at breakfast will compare the room's coffee against that standard, not against a generic hotel, so the inconsistency costs more here than it would at a hotel without the concept. The fix is modest, since a Spanish coffee or tea offer extends an existing supplier relationship rather than creating a new concept.

**Decision 3: The Level and public co-working.** Business Traveller (2024) reports ten workspaces from £40 a day, with 10% off at Arado and '35 Bar. Ansoff (1957) would call this related diversification, selling a new offer into familiar markets and assets. Drucker (1985) sees innovation in under-used resources, and a quiet lounge fits that description. Olsen, West and Tse (2008) add that diversification should reinforce the core. The lounge and communal table (Fig. 4) and '35 Bar (Fig. 8) show a flexible work-and-dine space with a second outlet. The verdict is that this is a smart use of space and a sensible cross-selling lever, but usage and revenue are unknown, and the trade press source may carry partner input. The evidence supports intent, not performance, which is why Recommendation 4 tracks it. The ten desks are also a small share of a hotel with several hundred rooms, so the initiative is better read as a way of drawing local professionals into Arado and '35 Bar than as a major revenue line. Without redemption data the discount cannot be judged either a sensible cross-selling lever or a giveaway to people who would have bought anyway.

**Decision 4: the premium badge.** Brand Finance coverage reports that premium and luxury hotels make up about 65% of Meliá's portfolio, and S&P coverage cites a sustainability ranking (Journal des Palaces, 2026; Breaking Travel News, 2026) [VERIFY BOTH]. These are corporate or ranking claims, not audits. Zeithaml (1988) and Xia et al. (2004) suggest that a premium badge raises the standard against which every detail is judged, particularly at £300 rather than £117. Manaktola and Jauhari (2007) find that guests' green attitudes do not always translate into choices, so sustainability should be supporting value, not the lead. The verdict is that the badge is a promise the hotel must now prove room by room, and the group claims say little about this property. A brand that wanted guests to credit its green record would need visible, repeated cues, and a single organic product offers little. The decision is best read as group-level brand positioning with limited evidence of guest-level effect at this property.

## 5. How well is it working?

The three evidence types agree on the pattern. The visit, the documents and the reviews all show strength in public spaces, breakfast and staff, and weakness in condition and consistency. Observation shows the intended design, the reviews show lived experience, and the documents show what the hotel says it has done. Where they disagree, the report gives more weight to repeated guest accounts than to a single promotional claim. They disagree on scale: the visit and photographs show small, visible faults, whereas the press material promises that the hotel exceeds expectations. Triangulation here is a credibility check, not statistical proof.

The review sample is indicative, not representative (Litvin et al., 2008). Across 36 reviews [CONFIRM], touchpoints drew [X] positive and [Y] negative mentions, and the largest negative cluster concerned [INSERT FROM YOUR CHART] (Appendix 4). The chart cannot show how common these experiences are among all guests, only which themes recur in the sample.

Appendix 5 sets promise against proof. Two rows matter most. The promise of a consistent Meliá standard at breakfast sits against a review reporting breakfast moved offsite after an electrical fault, rated red, although it rests on one review and needs corroboration. The premium promise sits against a tarnished lift door and a dusty grille (Figs. 1 and 6), rated amber. Both are small things, and at a £300 rate they are expensive, because Zeithaml (1988) implies the guest paying more expects more. Using Kano's logic, these are must-be failures, and the lens explains why they outweigh the delight delivered by Arado.

Zomerdijk and Voss (2010) treat the journey as a chain of touchpoints, and the weakest touchpoint shapes the memory, so one tarnished lift can outweigh an excellent breakfast. Staff are named in positive reviews, which is a clear strength. Schneider and Bowen (1993) link staff experience to guest satisfaction, but the causality is weakly supported, so this is context, not proof.

In Grönroos's terms, the hotel's functional quality (staff and process) is strong and its technical quality (condition) is uneven. The strategy works at the headline touchpoints and underdelivers on consistency. That is encouraging for management, because failures of technical quality respond to routine checklist control rather than capital spend. The findings therefore describe maintenance of a largely successful repositioning, not a case for rethinking it.

## 6. Recommendations

All costs and timings are the author's estimates. Each recommendation is tied to evidence and a lens.

**R1. Must-be sweep and weekly guest-eyes walk.** Run a 30-day sweep of lifts, grilles, corridors and room details, then a weekly 15-minute manager walk with a short checklist. Figs. 1, 6 and 7 show the faults, Kano et al. (1984) rank must-be failures above missing delight, and Turley and Milliman (2000) show the setting shapes judgement. Measure weekly checklist pass rate and monthly review mentions of condition faults. Estimate: low cost, 1 to 3 months. The risk is that plaster and cornice repairs may need listed-building consent, so start with cleaning and metal polishing.

**R2. Breakfast continuity and recovery playbook.** Write an outage plan with a backup service area, a scripted apology, a standard make-good such as a drink at '35 Bar, and a same-day manager follow-up. The offsite-breakfast review is the evidence. Smith et al. (1999) support recovery, and McCollough et al. (2000) explain why a backup plan is also needed. Measure time to first response, the share of affected guests offered a make-good, and reply time to related reviews. Estimate: low cost, about one month. The risk is that compensation becomes an expectation.

**R3. "Spanish morning" in-room kit.** Replace instant coffee with a Spanish coffee or tea option and add a local-products card pointing to Arado and '35 Bar, piloted on one floor. Fig. 5 contrasts with Figs. 2 and 3, Kim and Mauborgne (2004) support carrying the distinctive element through, and Treacy and Wiersema (1993) are the counterpoint: choose where to be excellent. Measure in-room F&B mentions, minibar spend and card use. Estimate: low to medium cost, three months. The risk is cost per room across more than 500 rooms, so pilot results decide roll-out.

**R4. Track The Level.** Give each day pass a unique code for the 10% discount, then report weekly passes sold, redemption rate and spend per pass-holder. Figs. 4 and 8 and Business Traveller (2024) show the offer, and Ansoff (1957) and Drucker (1985) imply a new use of space is only a good bet if watched. Measure occupancy of the ten workspaces and average spend. Estimate: low cost, about two months. The risk is cannibalised full-price spend.

Priority, by impact against effort: Taken together, R1 to R3 address must-be reliability and expectation, which the evidence shows are the main sources of dissatisfaction, and they draw on different teams, so they can run in parallel. R2 and R1 first, as the cheapest and most visible; R4 next; R3 last, since it needs the most money and testing. The order should be adjusted once the review sample shows which touchpoint causes most complaints. Success should be judged after one quarter against the stated indicators, using the same review-coding frame so that results are comparable with this study's baseline, and any item that shows no movement should be redesigned before more money is spent. A 90-day timeline is in Appendix 6.

## 7. Conclusion

Meliá White House largely delivers its premium promise where guests look first, in public spaces, breakfast and staff, but under-delivers on condition and consistency, which matters more at £300 than at £117. These findings rest on one hosted visit, perceptions-based evidence and a small, self-selected review sample. The hotel should first fix breakfast continuity and its must-be basics, because they are cheap, fast and protect the investment already made.
