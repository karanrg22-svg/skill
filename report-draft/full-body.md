## 1. Introduction

Meliá White House is a Grade II listed 1930s building on Albany Street, London, operated by Meliá since 1999. A renovation that Meliá reports at £40m over five years was described as complete in April 2023 (Hospitality Net, 2023; Business Traveller, 2024), and the hotel now positions itself as premium.

The aim is to judge how well this strategy delivers a consistent premium guest experience, using food and beverage (F&B) as the main lens, and to recommend practical improvements. The objectives are to:

1. build a short theory toolkit from service quality, servicescape, expectation and strategic fit literature;
2. examine the hotel's key decisions: repositioning, F&B concept and diversification;
3. test those decisions against visit evidence and secondary sources together; and
4. recommend improvements that are costed, timed and measurable.

The argument is that the public spaces and breakfast deliver the premium promise, while the gaps lie in small, repeated touchpoints. Those gaps matter more at a quoted rate near £300 than at roughly £117, and they are cheap and quick to fix. The report proceeds by building a theory toolkit, analysing four decisions, testing them against evidence and then recommending low-cost actions that protect the investment already made.

## 2. Approach and methods

The work follows a consultancy sequence of entry, diagnosis, data collection, feedback and recommendation, as set out by Kubr (2002). Block (2011) frames consulting as getting expertise used, which suits a short advisory piece. Schein's (1999) process consultation aims to help the client see the problem itself, which fits poorly with a single-day visit and no ongoing client relationship. This report therefore leans on Block and Kubr and treats Schein as a limit on what a one-visit diagnosis can claim.

Primary data came from non-participant observation and photographs on a hosted tour on 9 September 2025 [CONFIRM DATE AGAINST YOUR NOTES], and from an informal two-way session with hotel staff. That session mostly concerned careers, and the tour was hosted, so what was seen is a managed version of the hotel. Staff remarks are paraphrased and are not presented as company policy. No identifiable staff or guests appear in any photograph.

Secondary data comprised Camden Council planning documents, Meliá press material, trade press (Business Traveller, 2024) and online reviews from three platforms. Triangulation across these sources follows Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill (2019); Table A in Appendix 2 summarises each source's strength and weakness.

The limits are plain. There was one visit and a staged tour. Management strategy and financial data were unavailable. Corporate sources are promotional, and reviews are self-selected, so using three platforms reduces platform bias but not self-selection bias. Sources also conflict on facts: Meliá's press material gives 581 bedrooms, while a Camden Council document gives 548 (Camden Council, 2025). Both are reported here rather than one being chosen silently. A further caution concerns the date of evidence: Business Traveller's co-working article appeared in 2024, so prices and availability may have changed, and the staff quotation of about £300 is a verbal figure rather than a published tariff. Findings are therefore framed as indicative, and each claim is tied to the source that supports it.

## 3. Theory toolkit

Six theories are used because each connects to a specific decision or observed gap.

**Gaps Model.** Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry (1985) locate service failure in gaps between expectations, management perceptions, specifications, delivery and communication. It is the master framework because the problems found sit in Gap 3 (delivery against standard) and Gap 4 (promise against delivery). Its weakness is that it is provider-side and says little about how guests feel, so the guest-side theories below complement it. A further limitation is that the model treats each gap as separable, whereas in a hotel a single maintenance lapse can simultaneously breach the specification, the delivery and the promise made in marketing. Used carefully, it still gives the report a disciplined way to classify each finding rather than simply listing complaints.

**SERVQUAL and SERVPERF.** Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry (1988) measure quality as perception minus expectation across five dimensions, whereas Cronin and Taylor (1992) contend that perceptions alone predict quality better, and that the dimensions shift between settings. Only perceptions are available here, drawn from observation and reviews, so a SERVPERF-style approach is taken, with the five dimensions retained as a coding frame. This choice also avoids pretending to measure expectations that were never collected. Tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance and empathy are therefore used to sort evidence, not to calculate a score, and the report claims patterns rather than measured quality.

**Servicescape.** Bitner (1992), building on Kotler's (1973) atmospherics, argues that physical surroundings shape guest judgement and employee behaviour. The theory was developed for controlled settings. Heritage complicates it: Timothy and Boyd (2003) show that visible age can signal authenticity, though it can equally read as neglect. At a premium rate, tolerance for wear should fall, so the question is whether cornices and oak outweigh a tarnished lift door and a dusty grille. Servicescape also suggests that guests infer the hotel's care for them from the condition of surfaces they touch daily, which is why lifts and corridors receive more weight here than their size would suggest.

**Expectation-disconfirmation.** Oliver (1980) holds that satisfaction depends on whether performance exceeds expectation. Expectations are hard to measure and change over time, which limits the theory. It matters here because staff quoted rooms from about £300, while booking sites show prices nearer £117 [VERIFY PRICE AND DATE], so guests arrive with very different reference points. The same stay can therefore delight one guest and disappoint another without any change in what the hotel delivers, which helps explain why reviews of the same building diverge.

**Differentiation and strategic fit.** Porter (1996) argues that strategy rests on activities that reinforce one another, and he separates strategy from operational effectiveness, which he treats in Porter (1985) as a source of cost or differentiation advantage. The "stuck in the middle" claim is contested. Fit is used to test whether the Mediterranean concept holds across every touchpoint, including those guests cannot see from the lobby. A concept that is vivid in public and absent in private suggests the hotel has designed a front-of-house story rather than a consistent set of activities, which is the distinction Porter draws.

**Service recovery.** Hart, Heskett and Sasser (1990) argue that a well-handled failure can build loyalty, and Tax, Brown and Chandrashekaran (1998) tie this to perceived fairness. Recovery cannot replace reliability, and fairness judgements differ between guests. The theory is used to assess how the breakfast disruption was handled. Because the evidence is a single review, the theory is applied cautiously, and the recommendation that follows is framed as a protocol rather than a verdict on past performance.

## 4. Analysis of four hotel decisions

**Decision 1: a five-year repositioning while trading.** Meliá reports £40m invested over five years while the hotel stayed open (Hospitality Net, 2023). Barney (1991) would treat a Grade II listed, star-shaped building as a rare and hard-to-imitate resource, which suggests a genuine advantage. Teece, Pisano and Shuen (1997) add that renovating while trading is a dynamic capability, because revenue continues. Yet trading through works has a cost: reviews that mention faults or disruption [INSERT YOUR COUNT FROM TABLE D] suggest guests absorbed it. Planning documents show work continued after the main programme, including refurbishment of Wing A and a basement conversion (Camden Council, 2025). The judgement is that the heritage asset justifies the investment, but phased delivery exposed Gap 3: promised standards were not always met while works continued. Resource-based reasoning also warns that heritage is only an advantage if it is maintained, since a listed building that looks tired converts a rare asset into a liability. The renovation therefore creates an obligation to sustain standards, not just to reach them once.

**Decision 2: the Mediterranean F&B concept.** Arado, a buffet and Spanish wines and cava form a coherent offer. In Porter's (1996) terms, strategic fit means activities that reinforce each other, and the buffet counter with GMED oils and Real Crisps and the organic Codorníu cava on ice (Figs. 2 and 3) show that fit where guests can see it. Kotler, Bowen and Makens (2017) make the positioning point that an offer must be distinctive and consistently delivered. Breakfast is the most praised item in the reviews examined. The concept breaks in the room, where instant coffee (Fig. 5) contradicts the Spanish, premium identity. The judgement is that this is a Gap 4 promise-delivery mismatch and a fit failure, not simply a housekeeping lapse, because it breaks the concept at the point of private consumption. Managers should note that the fix is modest: a Spanish coffee or tea offer extends an existing supplier relationship rather than creating a new concept. The strength of the concept elsewhere makes the room lapse more noticeable, since a guest who has enjoyed Spanish oils and cava at breakfast will compare the room's instant coffee against that standard rather than against a generic hotel.

**Decision 3: diversification through The Level and public co-working.** Business Traveller (2024) reports ten workspaces from £40 a day, with 10% off at Arado and '35 Bar. This is enterprise behaviour: a new income stream from underused space, with cross-selling into F&B, consistent with Enz's (2010) account of strategy and new revenue. The lounge and communal table (Fig. 4) and '35 Bar (Fig. 8) show a flexible work-and-dine space and a distinct second outlet. The judgement is that this is a sound use of space, but no usage or revenue figures were available, so it cannot be called a success. The evidence supports intent, not performance, and management should track desk bookings and discount redemptions to learn whether the scheme draws new spend into the restaurant and bar, and the trade press source may carry partner input. The ten desks are also a small proportion of a hotel with several hundred rooms, so the initiative is better read as a way of drawing local professionals into Arado and '35 Bar than as a major revenue line. If cross-selling is the aim, the 10% discount is a sensible lever, but its effect cannot be judged without redemption data.

**Decision 4: group premium strategy and sustainability.** Brand Finance coverage reports that premium and luxury hotels make up about 65% of Meliá's portfolio (Journal des Palaces, 2026) [VERIFY AND KEEP ATTRIBUTED AS A CORPORATE CLAIM]. Han, Hsu and Sheu (2010) show an attitude-behaviour gap: guests favour green hotels more readily than they pay for them. The only visit evidence is organic cava. The judgement is that sustainability is supporting value, not the lead, and that group claims come from promotional sources and should be treated accordingly. A hotel that wanted guests to value sustainability would need visible, repeated cues, and a single organic product offers little. The decision is therefore best read as brand positioning at group level, with limited evidence of guest-level effect at this property.

## 5. How well is it working?

The evidence converges on a pattern: strength where guests look first, weakness in repeated detail. Photographs, staff dialogue, planning and press documents, and reviews point the same way, though all carry bias, as Saunders et al. (2019) warn. Triangulation here is a cross-check on credibility, not a statistical proof. Observation shows the intended design, reviews show the lived experience, and the planning and press documents show what the hotel says it has done. Where these disagree, the report gives more weight to repeated guest accounts than to a single promotional claim.

Review findings from the 40-review sample are summarised in Table D (Appendix 4) [INSERT YOUR TOTALS: e.g. "Tangibles drew X positive and Y negative mentions"]. Breakfast and staff attract the most praise. Reviews that name individual staff are a clear positive. The service-profit chain (Heskett et al., 1994) would link satisfied staff to guest loyalty, but its causal links are weakly supported, so this is noted only as context. What can be said is narrower: where staff are named, they are named favourably, which indicates that frontline service is a strength the hotel can build on while it fixes the physical details.

Table E (Appendix 5) sets brand promise against observation. Three rows matter most. First, the promise that the hotel exceeds expectations sits against a tarnished lift door and a dusty gym grille (Figs. 1 and 6), a medium Gap 3. Second, the Mediterranean identity sits against instant coffee in the room (Fig. 5), a low-to-medium Gap 4. Third, the promise of a consistent Meliá standard sits against a review reporting breakfast moved offsite after an electrical fault, a high Gap 3. This last item rests on a single review and needs corroboration.

Price sharpens every gap. Oliver (1980) implies that guests paying near £300 expect more than guests paying £117 [VERIFY], so the same worn lift reads as character at one price and neglect at the other (Bitner, 1992; Timothy and Boyd, 2003). Lemon and Verhoef (2016) add that guests remember the weakest touchpoint rather than the best, which means one tarnished lift can outweigh an excellent breakfast. This is the strongest argument for treating small fixes as strategic: they protect the investment already made in the public spaces. Overall, the strategy works at the headline touchpoints and underdelivers on consistency. In Gaps Model terms, the hotel has probably set the right standards, since the public spaces show clear design intent, but it does not yet deliver them evenly. The weakness lies mainly in Gap 3, the operational link between specification and delivery, with a smaller Gap 4 around the Mediterranean promise in rooms. That is encouraging for management because Gap 3 failures respond to routine, checklist-based control rather than capital spend. It also means the findings should be read as maintenance of an already successful repositioning, not a case for rethinking it.

## 6. Recommendations

All costs and timings are the author's estimates. They are ranked by impact against effort.

**1. Breakfast service-recovery protocol (low cost, about one month).** Train staff to respond to disruption and set clear compensation. The evidence is the offsite-breakfast review, and Hart et al. (1990) and Tax et al. (1998) show that fair recovery can turn failure into goodwill. Measure complaint resolution rate and review response time. The risk is staff time. Because the evidence for the disruption is limited to one review, the protocol should be piloted and its use logged, so that management can judge whether the problem recurs.

**2. Weekly guest-facing condition check (low cost, one to three months).** Audit lifts, grilles and corridors with a simple tangibles checklist. The evidence is Figs. 1, 6 and 7, and the theory is SERVQUAL tangibles and Bitner (1992). Measure the audit pass rate and the tangibles score in reviews. The risk is that some repairs need listed-building consent. Cosmetic items such as polishing brass or cleaning grilles need no consent, so most of the check can begin immediately.

**3. Clear room-tier descriptions (low cost, one to two months).** Describe tiers so expectations match price. The evidence is the £300 against £117 gap, and the theory is Oliver (1980) and yield management (Kimes, 1989). Measure reviews mentioning value. The risk is possible revenue dilution if clarity lowers bookings in lower tiers.

**4. Bring Arado into the rooms (low-to-medium cost, about three months).** Offer Spanish coffee or tea and a short local-products card. The evidence is Figs. 5 and 2, and the theory is Porter's (1996) fit and Pine and Gilmore (1998) on staging experience. Measure in-room F&B mentions and minibar spend. The risk is cost per room across a large room stock, so a pilot floor should come first. A pilot also provides before-and-after review evidence, which this study lacked. The Arado-in-rooms idea is deliberately ranked last because it is the only action that changes the product and carries a recurring unit cost.

The priority order is 1, 2, 3, 4: the first is cheapest and most visible, the last needs most money and testing. Taken together, the first three actions address reliability and expectation, which the evidence shows are the main sources of dissatisfaction, and can run in parallel because they draw on different teams: guest services, housekeeping and engineering, and revenue management. Success should be judged after one quarter against the stated key performance indicators, using the same review-coding frame so that results are comparable with this study's baseline.

## 7. Conclusion

The strategy largely delivers where guests look first, in the public spaces, breakfast and staff, but it underdelivers on consistency. The small repeated touchpoints are where the premium promise leaks, and they are inexpensive to fix. These findings rest on one hosted visit, perceptions only and self-selected reviews, so they indicate a pattern rather than prove it. Further work should test the pattern against a larger review set and management data.
